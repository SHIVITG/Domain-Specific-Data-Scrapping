unending – and for very good reason. Here, Jonathan Anderson talks broken Britain, cult bags and why he refuses to work with ‘yes’ people… I never went into this industry to be mediocre – that would just be depressing. I went into this industry to be the best,” says Jonathan Anderson. He admits it’s not very ‘British’ to say this and acknowledges it could be perceived as arrogant, but this is his focus and ultimately what drives him. “It’s like running a marathon; you don’t enter the race if you don’t want to come first. I do see fashion like that; it is about winning. I know it sounds kind of ridiculous, but if you want to do good collections and you want to make a successful business, then you have to be out to win.”I first met Jonathan at the beginning of his race, when he worked out of a windowless studio on Shacklewell Lane in Dalston, East London, and lived close by in a flat above a computer-game shop, furnished with chairs he’d picked out of a skip. Back then, he had a team of four, fitted his designs on a boy, and his debut A/W ’11 womenswear collection, with its hairy hiking boots and silk paisley kilts over matching trousers (inspired by Granny Anderson’s penchant for wearing aprons over trackie bottoms), had been received with rave reviews. We went for lunch and he told me that he worked 24/7 – including Christmas Day. Then in his twenties, he was all lanky limbs with floppy sandy hair and a milky County Derry complexion. Initially, he’d set out wanting to become an actor and trained at the Studio Theatre in Washington DC, but decided acting wasn’t for him and had moved back to Dublin, picking up work in the department store Brown Thomas.A chance encounter there, over the Prada display rack, with the late Manuela Pavesi (Miuccia Prada’s then right-hand woman) inspired him to enrol at the London College of Fashion, where he studied menswear, graduating in 2005 before returning to work with Pavesi as a visual merchandiser for Prada. He launched his career with JW Anderson menswear in 2008 and, two years later, due to popular demand,  added womenswear. Four years after our first encounter, in 2016, I interviewed him again. By this point, industry darling, trend provoker and fashion agitator, he was working out of his new East End headquarters, all concrete floors, white-washed walls and interesting sculptures. He’d clocked up an impressive array of accolades: Winning both British men’s and womenswear designer of the year at the Fashion Awards in 2015, which was unheard of, as well as nailing commercial collaborations with Topshop (which sold out) and Uniqlo (which is ongoing, as is Converse). At just 29, the same year he collaborated with Donatella Versace on her ‘Versus’ line, he won the backing of Bernard Arnault, chairman of LVMH, the luxury goods conglomerate, and the richest man in Europe, who bought a minority stake in JW Anderson. In fact, Arnault had such faith in Jonathan, he also handed him the LVMH-owned Loewe brand, Spain’s answer to Hermès, to creatively direct. Aside from the pressure of overseeing two brands – which he seemed to relish – it was clear that what set him apart from his contemporaries wasn’t just his ability to anticipate the moment in clothes, but that he was equally conversant in commercial. As he said then, “You can’t be a fantasist. It’s impossible. This is a rag trade, you’ve gotta sell it. Quicker. Fast. Faster than ever. It would be awful to produce a product that nobody wanted.” Clearly, that hasn’t been a problem. Those of us who have invested in a JW Anderson bag (bolt pierced, anchor logoed, the ‘Keyts’, or the ‘Cap’) or a Loewe one (‘Puzzle’, ‘Hammock’, ‘Gate’, ‘Bow’, ‘Flamenco’, ‘Amazona’, ‘Barcelona’, ‘Elephant’, ‘Basket’, to name just the most memorable) can appreciate his prodigious talent for multiple bag hits – the holy grail of any luxury goods brand. As for the clothes, they often hit the spot where the retired Phoebe Philo (of old Céline) left off – judging merely by the volume of JW Anderson and Loewe worn at the shows by both flamboyant street style professionals and low-key journalists. “Recently, I heard someone say I’m less hot-headed,” muses Jonathan, now 34, whose reputation for having a fiery temper precedes him. So, is he? “I think so, yes. When I first took on Loewe and had my own brand, there was anxiety, because you’re trying to prove (yourself) all the time and that comes with making harsh decisions. A lot of people don’t like that, they’re petrified of people who think on the spot or have a certain viewpoint. For me, I have a corridor that I work within, very accurately. I am very black or white.”Today, once again, we are meeting in his new East End headquarters – significantly larger than the last – where he has been installed since February. It is now July, and his studio is still in a state of moving-in with his art and objects yet to be unpacked. You can see why: He lives in London and Paris (where the Loewe design studio is based) and shuttles between both cities every week, plus Madrid (for Loewe’s bag development and manufacturing) every month. “I need a five-day week and I need it to be full. The minute I have a gap in the day, I can lose the momentum and then I’m done. And I need to go quick. I can’t do a long meeting. I need people to come prepared, and if they’re not prepared then I won’t do it.” This must be music to the ears of his CEO, Jenny Galimberti, who started with JW Anderson last September. I ask her what impresses her most about Jonathan: “He’s fantastic to work with; a constant source of ideas. Intellectually challenging, multifaceted, and with a huge range of conversation topics, from his passion for ceramics to running our business, from politics and macro-economics to really just about anything...we never get bored.” How does Jonathan himself approach being one of the few designers in the industry straddling two businesses – a luxury giant with 113 stores and his own brand, which will open its first flagship in London’s Soho early next year.He says he sees Loewe as a ‘cultural brand’ and JW as a ‘cultural agitator’; he pictures his Loewe woman in a lifestyle space and his JW woman as someone who “wants us to push the needle”. With 18 collections a year to come up with – 10 for Loewe and eight for JW – work is a military operation. He splits the year into four, working on two different ‘chapters’ every quarter, “very methodically, always nine months in advance, because I like to work layers and layers and layers into it. I don’t work last minute, it can’t be stress, it has to be very plotted out.” He still thrives on the pressure, both critical and commercial: “There is definitely more pressure on designers who are now judged on their sales performance as well as their creativity. For me, it’s like adrenaline, I need that level of heightened-ness to make me feel like I have to continue to prove.” Yet despite being in it to win it, he refuses to allow himself to think he’s winning: “I never feel that it’s there – if it was, I don’t know if I’d do it anymore. What are the pattern and motif trends dominating the market today?  SHEFALEE VASUDEV: Frankly, my observations do not suggest a marked shift from floral, paisley, shikargaha (the forest hunt with animal and bird motifs) or jamdani jaal patterns to what is termed by some as Victorian or geometric. There is, in fact, a visible co-existence of new and old design vocabulary with a stronger tilt towards the traditional instead of the modern. The loud and persistent presence of banarasi weaves across retail segments and markets, which is mirrored across media, social media, at weddings or traditional events suggests a re-engagement with older banarasi forms, not so much with the new. Ways of draping a traditional banarasi sari may have turned a new page, but the essential weave doesn’t reveal a significant departure. What has changed is that the work of designers has made the weave lighter, its fluidity, wearability and applicability for tailored garments has been considerably enhanced.In this context, it is important to mention some notable experiments with banarasi. Abraham & Thakore’s ‘Shaadi Redux’ collection of 2013, with contemporary chattai (mat) weaves woven in collaboration with Ekaya, which yielded large squares and intersecting lines and lighter than usual textiles, Sanjay Garg’s interpretation of stripes, textured, block-coloured banarasi weaves or monkey motifs from his ‘Monkey Business’ collection of 2016, Rajesh Pratap Singh’s work with the Banaras loom without any ethnic references for his collection with ‘Tencel’ last year are just a couple of the newer interpretations. There are others – Hemang Agrawal’s work with modernising banarasi is significant in this conversation. But, the non-designer market, as well as large aspects of designer work, is dominated by the traditional. It was upheld in garments chosen by actors like Deepika Padukone (the red dupatta she wore on her arrival in India after her Italy wedding), Anushka Sharma’s much-discussed red banarasi sari for her wedding reception from the Sabyasachi atelier – it had round traditional ashrafi (coin) motifs on it. Or, the many iterations sold by stores like Ekaya, L’Affaire, among others, are stronger versions of classical banarasi designs. Ritu Kumar’s revival of the banarasi for her ‘Varanasi Weaves’ collection of 2015, which she said took her a year and more to get a bunch of completely handwoven saris, weren’t ‘modern’. They were traditional in design.   G: The banarasi has many myriad variations, what type, according to you, has been commercially successful over the past few years and why? SV: The silk brocades seem to have found the most customers and curious explorers in the last few years – both in terms of yardage for garments, dupattas as well as the timeless brocade saris.  G: A banarasi sari can easily be deemed as the holy grail of saris, given its painstaking production process as well as its association with matrimony, in a fashion landscape dominated by quantity versus quality. How does this art form maintain its integrity?  SV: The banarasi’s survival or, what you would call, maintenance of integrity as a form of textile art, has only been possible with the intervention of textile designers and experts. In its retail format, it is more commercial than artistic. The work of senior textile expert Rahul Jain, banarasi weaves in real gold and silver zari commissioned by designer duo Swati and Sunaina, designer Hemang Agrawal’s admirable new interpretations or the design interventions by Sanjay Garg and Abraham & Thakore have elevated the banarasi sari from a wearable handloom to textile art. Besides this, banarasi textiles have been traditionally woven for Buddhist monasteries – the weavers of Banaras continue to do this, and many of these interpretations are artistic.   G: From your personal observations, what are the largest challenges faced by artisans dabbling in this specialised ï¬ÂÂÂÂeld?  SV: GST imposed on weavers and craftspeople without due consideration of the income slabs they fall in as well as issues of copyright violation are among the top concerns. ‘Banaras brocade and saris’ – defined thus, have had a Geographical Indication (GI) tag since 2009, but replicas and fakes are woven on the machine loom everywhere in India. You can find a banarasi in Surat and in local markets of cities, these replicas created on the machine in Chinese silk are available from Rs. 500 onwards, per sari. This greatly threatens the demand for an authentic handwoven weave in pure silk. Also, while the thrust by the current government has fruitfully redirected the attention to Banaras weaves and saris, the overall life quality of Banaras weavers, their wages, working conditions, electricity, the pricing logic of what they get from traders or middlemen, rewards and pensions, health insurance, or the assurance of education for their children haven’t really changed as much as it should have. The recent surging popularity of the banarasi weave has also brought in its wake questions of inequality – what needs to be documented is how much the weaver has gained in terms of recognition and fame, earnings and other takeaways. Many benefits have unequally gone to designers, traders and entrepreneurs working with Banaras clusters with fewer gains circling back to the original creators.    G: The banarasi has been the focus of a lot of attention over the past couple of years. Is there a reason why this particular weave has been garnering attention even from the likes of the Ministry of Textiles?  SV: It has clearly been a conscious decision on the part of the BJP government to focus on Banaras textiles. It was one of the electoral promises of Prime Minister Narendra Modi to give attention to the handloom industry in Banaras, which is his constituency. The Ministry of Textiles has been enabling the fulfilment of that promise, and this thrust has indeed benefitted the cluster.   G: Where does reinterpretation take this weave moving forward? SV: The banarasi– like the chanderi for different reasons – is ahead in the handloom market. Both in the interpretations, it has seen in mainstream fashion as well as in the mass retail market. Here’s a quick guide on all the trends we loved and what you should be wearing this upcoming wedding season to make a glam statement.  Kiara Advani turned showstopper for Amit Aggarwal's opening show at ICW 2019  Apt for the contemporary Indian bride, Amit Aggarwal’s ‘Lumen’ featured bright jewel-toned garments in luxe fabric and layered pieces.										Pankaj & NidhiKiara Advani turned showstopper for Amit Aggarwal's opening show at ICW 2019   Pankaj & Nidhi presented their debut couture collection, ‘Mosaiq’, using their signature techniques such as tropunto quilting on luxe fabrics like silks and satins. If you’re a fuss-free bride, you should definitely look at adding it to your wedding trousseau.										Suneet Varma   Designer Suneer Varma’s couture collection was a play of traditional motifs and applique work on a wide range of fabrics such as silk, satin and georgette that’s suitable for every occassion.										Falguni & ShaneKiara Advani turned showstopper for Amit Aggarwal's opening show at ICW 2019   Falguni & Shane’s collection, ‘Bonjour Amer’, was a celebration of the extravagant and eclectic landscaped of Jaipur in vivid colours that are bound to enrich your festive wear. 										Shyamal and BhumikaKiara Advani turned showstopper for Amit Aggarwal's opening show at ICW 2019   Shyamal and Bhumika put out their collection, ‘Renaissance Muse’, inspired by 15th century European aesthetics re-imagined in Indian bridal couture. The varied pieces included kalidaar panelled lehengas and shararas that you should add to your occasion wear wardrobe, stat.										Gaurav GuptaKiara Advani turned showstopper for Amit Aggarwal's opening show at ICW 2019   Gaurav Gupta’s collection, ‘Undercurrent’, saw the designer’s signature sculpting technique combined with fringe gowns and billowing lehengas that will help you make a statement this season.										Tarun TahilianiTarun Tahiliani’s collection, ‘Bloom’, had classic pieces, tone-on-tone lehengas, anarkalis and saris sans the heaviness of traditional textiles, apt for the contemporary Indian bride.
